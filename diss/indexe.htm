1. Relevance of the Topic

A context-dependent spelling error detection and correction system based on language models is relevant due to the growing volume of textual information and the need to ensure its quality. Errors in texts can distort meaning, reduce trust in authors, and complicate data processing in machine learning systems. A context-dependent approach allows for consideration of the lexical environment, which improves correction accuracy compared to context-independent methods.

Statistics show that users make typos in 10–15% of cases, with context playing a key role in correct correction in 26% of cases. In search engines, for example, errors can lead to irrelevant results or lack of answers. In professional fields (scientific articles, legal documents), spelling accuracy is critically important for preserving legal validity and information reliability.

3. Brief Overview of the First Three Chapters

3.1 Basic Concepts and Technologies

Traditional spelling check systems, long considered the standard, were based on two main principles:

  • Dictionary method: The system checks each word in the text against a loaded dictionary (lexicon). Unknown words are flagged as potential errors. Main drawbacks: inability to detect contextual errors and the need for constant dictionary expansion to cover specialized terminology.
  • Statistical method based on N-grams (most often bigrams and trigrams): The model is trained on large text corpora, estimating the probability of word sequences. It can suggest corrections for unknown words by choosing a candidate that forms the most probable combination with surrounding words from the model's perspective. This method already considers local context, but its capabilities are limited by the N-word window and inability to capture deep semantic and syntactic connections.

The modern approach considered in this work is based on deep learning language models, which fundamentally change the paradigm. The correction task is formulated not as a dictionary search, but as a prediction or generation task of the most probable correct text in a given context.

3.2 Problems in Automation

Automatically making corrections to text without user involvement carries significant risks, both in terms of semantic distortion and preservation of authorial intent. In situations where context is ambiguous or subjective, algorithmic intervention can lead to undesirable distortions, up to interpreting the original content with opposite meaning. Furthermore, automatic correction can violate stylistic and genre features of the text, especially when working with literary, legal, or scientific documents. Therefore, an interactive strategy is preferable, where the system suggests corrections, but their final application is decided by the user. This approach significantly increases trust in the system and reduces the number of potentially destructive automatic changes.

One of the key problems in automating error detection in text is the inability of systems to reliably interpret the semantic level of content. Even with correct grammatical structure, a sentence can be absurd or incorrect from the standpoint of meaning. Simple rules and traditional algorithms cannot identify such inconsistencies, as they lack mechanisms for understanding logical connections between objects and actions. Effective detection of such errors requires integrating models with elements of semantic analysis and "common sense," such as transformer architectures enhanced with external knowledge bases or context-dependent reasoning mechanisms. However, even modern language models struggle with correct interpretation of complex and ambiguous contexts, significantly limiting the accuracy and reliability of automatic analysis.

3.3 Theoretical Foundations of Context-Dependent Spelling Correction

Historically, the task of automated spelling correction was solved within the paradigm of isolated lexical analysis, based on deterministic token matching against a reference dictionary. The key disadvantage of this approach is its fundamental inability to identify contextually irrelevant lexemes, where a graphemically correct word is semantically or syntactically mismatched with its environment.

Statistical methods, particularly N-th order language models (N-gram), partially mitigated this limitation by estimating joint probability of word sequences within a limited sliding window. However, their predictive capability is strictly limited by length N and does not extend to identifying complex syntagmatic and discursive dependencies.

A qualitative leap is associated with the introduction of deep neural language models into the field of natural language processing, architecturally based on the self-attention mechanism. Pre-trained models, formed as a result of training on masked language modeling (MLM) or autoregressive prediction tasks, encapsulate distributed semantic representations in their parameters. The ability of these architectures to model long-distance dependencies and contextual polysemy allows reformulating the correction task: not as a search task (by dictionary), but as a conditional generation or sequential classification task, where the target token is predicted based on the entire ensemble of contextual embeddings.

4. Results

During the work, a modular system was designed and implemented, with a fine-tuned Transformer architecture language model at its core.

The developed context-dependent spelling correction system was implemented based on the fine-tuned RuBERT model within a modular pipeline architecture, including sequential stages of error detection, generation of correction candidates, and their ranking using probabilistic assessment in the context of the entire sentence. The functional interface of the system is a REST API based on FastAPI.

Comparative analysis with baseline methods (dictionary rules) and modern analogues (SpellRuBERT, T5) showed that the developed system demonstrates comparable quality on general errors (F1=0.92, Accuracy=0.90 on RUSpell) and achieves better results in the target task of correcting contextual errors (F1=0.85, Accuracy=0.82). This confirms that the chosen approach based on fine-tuning a transformer model with a contextual candidate ranking mechanism is effective for resolving semantic ambiguity, for example, when correcting homophones and paronyms.
